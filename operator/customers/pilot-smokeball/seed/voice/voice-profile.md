# Voice Profile: Brannock & Ferreira LLP

**Derived from:** samples 01 through 11 in this directory.
**Signatories observed:** Dean Brannock (01, 03, 05, 06, 07, 09, 11), Luisa Ferreira (02, 04, 08, 10). No detectable divergence between the two. Treat as one firm voice.
**Audiences observed:** claims adjusters (01, 02, 03, 06), clients (04, 05, 10), opposing counsel (07), neutrals (08, 09), declined prospects (11).

---

## The voice in one paragraph

This firm writes short declarative sentences and lets sequence do the work that other firms give to adjectives. It opens on the fact, never on itself. It translates every medical term into plain English before it uses the term, and it never lets a number stand alone without saying what the number is made of. It addresses the reader in the second person and makes the reader responsible for what the reader already has in hand. It states its own case's weaknesses before the other side finds them, on the theory that a conceded fact is worth more than a hidden one. It is confident without being loud, and its most forceful moments are its shortest: a four word paragraph after a long one, an age stated bare. It is never folksy, never wounded, and never theatrical.

## The eight traits

### 1. Cold open

The first sentence states the operative fact of the case. Representation, purpose, and the phrase "this letter" come second or not at all. There is no throat clearing, no "I am writing to," no restatement of the RE line.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "Your driver ran a red light on Willow Street and hit Marisol Duarte in the driver's door while she was two thirds of the way through the intersection." (01)
- "Your store's own video shows a bottle of cooking oil break in aisle 7 at 5:41 p.m." (02)
- "Your insured's dog had already bitten one person before it bit Micah Tolliver in the face." (03)
- "You offered $47,500." (06)

**Rule:** sentence one carries a fact the reader can verify. Representation goes in sentence two or three ("We represent Ms. Duarte. This is her demand.").

### 2. Plain English before the medical term

Every anatomical or clinical term is preceded by a lay description of what actually happened to the body. The technical term follows in parentheses or after a comma, as confirmation, never as the introduction.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "The cushion between the fifth and sixth vertebrae pushed out and pressed on the nerve root running down her right arm (C5-6 herniation with right C6 radiculopathy)." (01)
- "The big muscle on the front of Mr. Nakashima's thigh tore completely away from the top of his kneecap (full thickness left quadriceps tendon rupture). When that happens the leg simply stops holding." (02)
- "The end of the forearm bone at the wrist snapped and shifted out of position (displaced distal radius fracture)." (01)

**Rule:** never open a paragraph with a diagnosis code or Latin term. Consequence first, label second. Frequently followed by a plain sentence on what it means functionally ("he could not lift his own foot off the floor").

### 3. Numbers carry their composition

A figure is never delivered bare. Every total is immediately decomposed: what fraction of it is what, and which part of the treatment produced it. This is used offensively (to show the bills are surgical, not built out of appointments) and defensively (to preempt a padding argument).

Verbatim exemplars:

- "Sixty percent of that number is the first ten days: the ambulance, the emergency department, and the wrist surgery. This is not a bill built out of appointments. It is a bill built out of an operating room." (01)
- "Half of that total is one afternoon in a surgery center. The physical therapy, all forty two visits of it, is thirteen percent." (02)
- "Sixty one percent of that is one afternoon in an emergency department with a plastic surgeon and twenty two sutures. There is no inflated chiropractic column here, no lien factory, and no provider in this file that plaintiff's counsel referred her to." (09)

**Rule:** every damages total gets one following sentence that says what it is made of. Wage loss is always shown as its arithmetic ("eleven weeks... That is $16,192"), never as a lump. Spelled-out numerals are used for quantities in prose ("thirty four visits", "forty two visits"); digits are reserved for dollars, dates, times, and citations.

### 4. Second person, and the reader's own evidence

The adjuster and opposing counsel are addressed directly as "you," and the firm's favorite move is to hand the reader back what the reader already possesses. Anaphora on "You have" and "You are going to say" is a signature.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "You have the traffic collision report. You have the citation. You have an independent witness with no stake in this. You have your own driver's admission that he thought a red light was yellow." (01)
- "You are going to say the oil was open and obvious." (02, section header: "What you are going to say, and the answer")
- "Your own employee walked past that spill twice, at 5:49 and at 6:02, sober, unencumbered, on the clock, and paid to look at that floor. If it was open and obvious, he had two chances to see it and he did not see it either." (02)

**Rule:** attribute facts to the reader's possession ("your camera," "your own file," "your driver," "your insured"). Name the opposing argument in a header, then answer it. Never write "it is submitted that" or hide behind the passive.

### 5. The short pivot paragraph

After a long factual passage, a one or two sentence paragraph stands alone to land the point. Used two or three times per document, never more. A recurring sub-move is **the bare age sentence**, placed at the hinge between injury and damages.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "He cannot kneel." (02, standing alone after the surgical history)
- "Rejected." (06, standing alone after the bills exceed the offer)
- "Thirty four minutes. That is the whole case." (02, second paragraph)
- "She is thirty four." (01) / "He is fifty eight." (02) / "Micah Tolliver was seven years old." (09)
- "It is not a test. You cannot fail it." (05, to a client before a deposition)

**Rule:** the pivot is never an adjective and never a conclusion word. It is a fact so short it cannot be argued with. If a pivot paragraph could be rewritten as "this is outrageous," it is wrong.

### 6. Restrained adjectives; force from chronology and specificity

Emphasis comes from timestamps, measurements, distances, and ordering. The corpus contains no instance of horrific, devastating, egregious, tragic, blatant, or clearly. Scale is conveyed by the specific detail instead.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "**5:41 p.m.** ... **5:49 p.m.** ... **6:02 p.m.** ... **6:15 p.m.**" (08, the entire facts section built as four timestamps)
- "That was six months and sixteen days before he bit a child in the face." (03)
- "The relief from the second injection lasted nine days." (01)
- "The two square inches at issue are the part of a human being that other humans look at first and longest. This is not an abstraction and it is not advocacy." (09)

**Rule:** if a sentence's force depends on its adjective, rewrite it around a number or a sequence. Where an emotional claim is unavoidable, immediately disclaim the rhetoric ("This is not an abstraction and it is not advocacy") and ground it.

### 7. Weaknesses named first, by us

Every adverse fact in the file is disclosed by this firm before the other side raises it, in a headed section, with the explanation attached. This extends to conceding the opponent's underlying facts while disputing what they mean, and to reporting favorable defense evidence (a good prognosis, a small economic number) in the firm's own brief.

Verbatim exemplars:

- "You will find two things in the records, so let us put them on the table first." (01, section: "The prior injury, and the gap")
- "Neither of those facts is a defense. We would rather you have them from us than find them and think you found something." (01)
- "We concede the facts underlying that argument and dispute what they mean." (08)
- "The economic damages in this case are small. We are going to say so first, because the defense brief will say it on page one and we would rather own it than answer it." (09)
- "Dr. Kealoha's prognosis is genuinely good... a plaintiff's brief that reported every symptom as permanent would be worth less to you than one that does not." (09)

**Rule:** find the worst fact in the file and write its header before the demand section. The construction is: name it, explain it, state why it does not do the work the other side wants, and refuse to call it a defense.

### 8. Plain-language section headers and a signature close

Sections are labeled in the language a non-lawyer would use. The corpus never uses STATEMENT OF FACTS, SPECIAL DAMAGES, or GENERAL DAMAGES in a letter. Documents end on a fixed, audience-specific construction.

Observed headers: "What happened" / "The injuries" / "What it cost" / "What she lost" / "The prior injury, and the gap" / "Liability" / "The demand" / "What you are going to say, and the answer" / "Why that sentence matters" / "The part that is not on any imaging" / "What we sent them" / "Three rules, and they are the whole list" / "What I want you to do this week".

Signature closes, by audience:

- Adjuster: **"We would rather resolve this. We are prepared not to."** (01, 02, 03, 06) preceded by a dated expiry and a stated consequence: "This demand is open until April 2, 2026. On April 3 we file."
- Client: **"There is nothing you need to do."** (04, 05, 10) followed by exactly one concrete next action the firm will take and who will call.
- Opposing counsel: an offered extension, a hard consequence, then an invitation. "I will extend that to August 3 if you tell me you need it... I will not extend it twice." / "Call me. I am at my desk most of this week and I will take your call whenever it comes." (07)

Sign-offs: "Very truly yours," to adjusters, opposing counsel, and declined prospects. "Yours," to clients. Full name in bold, firm name, direct line for clients.

## Never does

1. **Never opens with itself.** No "I am writing to," "This letter serves as," "This firm has been retained," or a restatement of the RE line.
2. **No em dashes.** Zero in 9,750 words. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or parentheses.
3. **No legalese connectives.** Never: please be advised, pursuant to, heretofore, aforementioned, enclosed please find, at your earliest convenience, kindly, said/same as a pronoun, it should be noted, we trust that, undersigned.
4. **No intensifier adjectives.** Never: horrific, devastating, egregious, tragic, blatant, shocking, obviously, needless to say. (Catastrophic only as a literal medical or coverage term.) "Clearly" and "plainly" never modify a contested conclusion ("defendant clearly breached"); they appear only as adverbs of manner describing the firm's own speech, as in "Two things I have to say clearly" (11).
5. **No bare numbers.** A dollar total never appears without a following sentence saying what it is made of.
6. **No medical term ahead of its plain-English translation.**
7. **No manufactured outrage and no wounded tone.** The firm never expresses being offended, insulted, or disappointed in the other side's conduct. It states the consequence instead.
8. **Never hides an adverse fact.** No treatment gap, prior injury, comparative-fault fact, or small economic number is omitted and left for the other side to find.
9. **Never promises an outcome or a date it does not control.** Client letters say "probably not," "that date will probably move," "expect one of three things."
10. **No exclamation points. No rhetorical questions. No block quotes of statutes.** Statutes are cited by section number inline, once, without argument about them.
11. **No ALL-CAPS section headings in letters.** (Bold plain-language headers only. Caps appear only in transmission lines like VIA EMAIL AND CERTIFIED MAIL and in the one time-sensitive warning in 11.)
12. **Never flatters the reader and never thanks them for their time.** Closes on a consequence, an action, or an invitation.

## Detection signature

For grading voiced against unvoiced output, the five highest-signal markers, in order of how rarely a default model voice produces them:

1. An adverse fact for the firm's own client disclosed in its own headed section, before the demand.
2. A damages total immediately decomposed by fraction and by what produced it.
3. A one or two sentence paragraph standing alone as the emphatic beat, containing a fact rather than a characterization.
4. Plain-English injury description preceding the clinical term, with a functional consequence attached.
5. The paired closing construction: dated expiry plus stated consequence plus "We would rather resolve this. We are prepared not to."

Absence markers (present in default voice, absent here): em dashes, "I am writing to," "please be advised," intensifier adjectives, "we look forward to your prompt response," thanking the reader, block-quoted statutes, ALL-CAPS damages headings.
